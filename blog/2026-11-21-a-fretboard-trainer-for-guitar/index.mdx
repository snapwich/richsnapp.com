Most scale charts for guitar show you one box of dots at a time. You learn the box, then the next box, and it is not
clear how they connect or why a mode is anything more than a different box. This tool shows you a scale as one pattern
of notes on the full neck, and it shows you which notes move when you change something.

## Scales, modes and keys

The idea behind the tool is that a small number of note patterns make most of the scales you will use. The major scale
pattern gives you seven modes. If you know where that pattern is on the neck, then Dorian or Mixolydian is the same
pattern with a different home note.

- **Scale** selects the pattern. There are eleven: major, harmonic minor, melodic minor, pentatonic, blues, double
  harmonic, whole tone, diminished, bebop dominant, bebop major and Persian.
- **Mode** selects the home note in that pattern, for example
  [D Dorian](/tools/fretboard?key=D&family=major&mode=dorian).
- **Key** moves the pattern up or down the neck.

A change of mode keeps the notes and moves the home note, and a change of key moves the full pattern. When you change
the scale, the neck shows which notes moved and where they went.
[A minor](/tools/fretboard?key=A&family=major&mode=aeolian&chord=off) to A harmonic minor is one note: G becomes G♯. You
can see that it is one note, and you can see each place on the neck where it occurs.

The labels on the notes can be note names, intervals, or nothing (for when you want to test yourself).

## Positions

A position is the part of the scale that is in reach of one hand. The tool has the two usual systems, CAGED and three
notes per string, and it also includes some classical and diagonal runs. You can step through the positions or drag the
highlighted band along the neck. The notes outside the position stay visible but dimmed, so you can always see how one
position connects to the next.

## Chords

The Chords row shows the chords of the key as triads or as 7th chords. Select one and the neck shows a shape for that
chord in the current position, with the chord tones marked on the scale. Move the position and the chord gets a new
shape there, so you can find the same chord in each part of the neck.

Below it, the Quality row lets you change a chord: make the V a V7, make a minor chord major, use a sus4, or put a
different note in the bass.

## Name a chord

Select notes on the neck, or type a shape such as `x32010`, and you get:

- **Names**: each name that those notes can have. `x32210` is Am with C in the bass, and it is also a C6 with no 5th.
  The tool lists the two.
- **Show as**: where that chord is. Each chip is a key (or a mode, or a different scale) that has the chord, with its
  roman numeral. Select a chip and the panel moves there.

If a song has a chord that is not in the key, Show as can tell you which mode or scale it comes from and which notes
change.

## Playback

The play button plays what the neck shows: the scale in the current position, or the selected chord as an arpeggio, as a
strum, or as the whole position. You can set the tempo, and you can change the chord or the position while it plays.
There is also a drone on the home note. A drone is a quick way to hear what a mode sounds like, because you hear each
note that you play against the home note.

## Your guitar

The frets on the neck are not all the same width. As on a real guitar, each fret is narrower than the one before it, so
the frets near the nut are wide and the high frets are close together. Most fretboard diagrams online draw each fret the
same width. With similar proportions, a shape on the screen looks like the shape under your fingers, and you can find a
position on your own guitar much more quickly.

Under the neck you can set the tuning (standard, E♭ or D), a capo up to fret 7, and the fret where the neck joins the
body (acoustic, classical or electric), so the picture agrees with the guitar in your hands.
